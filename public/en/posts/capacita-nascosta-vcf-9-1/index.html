The Hidden Capacity in the Data Center: Designing VMware Cloud Foundation 9.1 Before Buying New Hardware

A VMware cluster running at 65% utilization is not necessarily close to saturation. How to tell installed capacity from capacity you can actually use, before buying new hardware, with VMware Cloud Foundation 9.1.
July 31, 2026 · 14 min · Massimo C. | Translations:
  • It

Introduction#

This article draws on many years spent designing, transforming, and operating VMware infrastructures, and especially on Broadcom Architect training. Those courses go beyond individual product features: they emphasize architectural frameworks, traceable decisions, and the need to make requirements, constraints, risks, and trade-offs explicit. That is the method used here: start with the operational problem, measure it, and only then choose the technology.

In 2026, hardware availability is no longer the whole problem. Lead times keep slipping, costs keep rising, AI workloads keep pushing compute density up, and licensing models keep changing. Together these trends make a strategy built on simply adding servers harder to sustain.

When a VMware cluster hits 60-70% utilization, the usual reaction is to plan for new hosts. That reaction makes sense, but it’s often premature. The percentage alone doesn’t tell you which resource is actually constraining the cluster, how much capacity you can recover, how much space oversized VMs are occupying, or how much margin you need to reserve for resilience. It doesn’t even tell you whether the real constraint is compute, storage, network, or an architecture that isn’t ready to be managed as a private cloud yet.

The useful question isn’t how many servers to buy. It’s how much usable capacity you already have, and which architecture lets you use it without cutting into resilience or performance.

VMware Cloud Foundation 9.1 gives you the tools to approach this more rigorously, bringing compute, storage, network, security, and operations together in a single private platform. But adopting VCF doesn’t turn an inefficient infrastructure into an optimized one by itself. Method comes before technology.

Broadcom references: VMware Cloud Foundation 9.1 · Fleet Management

The False Problem of Utilization Percentage#

A cluster at 65% is not necessarily close to saturation. A cluster at 40% is not necessarily healthy. The same average can hide very different conditions: memory allocated but barely used, vCPUs assigned in excess, datastores approaching saturation, storage latency creeping up, VMs distributed unevenly, NUMA constraints, forgotten snapshots, workloads nobody needs anymore, clusters sized without accounting for the loss of one or more hosts.

The first mistake to avoid is confusing installed capacity with capacity you can actually use. In simplified terms:

Usable capacity = physical capacity − resilience reserve − overhead − inefficiencies − operational margin

Physical capacity is the number on the spec sheet. Usable capacity is what you can actually assign to workloads while maintaining the behavior you want during maintenance, failures, and demand spikes. It’s this second number, not the first, that should drive capacity planning and investment.

Broadcom reference: VCF Operations

Measuring the Real Limit#

Before adding new hosts, you need to establish which component is actually running out of margin. In many infrastructures compute looks saturated, but a closer look shows CPU and memory still have room, and the real limit is storage: insufficient capacity, a poor ratio between used and protected space, latency, throughput, long rebuild times, dependency on an array that has reached end of life.

In that scenario, adding servers produces a paradoxical result: compute capacity grows, the bottleneck stays exactly where it was, and the new hardware sits underused while the component that’s actually critical keeps limiting the whole platform.

Run the assessment separately across four domains. On compute, look at average and peak utilization, CPU Ready, Co-Stop, NUMA configuration, the ratio of vCPUs to physical cores, and the capacity you’d have left after losing one host. On memory, look beyond allocation to active and consumed memory, ballooning, compression, and swapping: a VM configured with 64 GB is not necessarily a VM that uses 64 GB. On storage, look at net capacity, space reserved for protection, latency, IOPS, throughput, growth, and rebuild times during faults and maintenance; VCF Operations collects the metrics you need to correlate consumption with the operating conditions of vSAN environments. On the network, a platform can have adequate CPU and storage and still be limited by undersized uplinks, non-redundant configurations, oversubscription, retransmissions, and high latency between domains or sites; VCF Operations dashboards help isolate these symptoms.

Only once this analysis is done can you tell whether you actually need more compute, more storage, a network redesign, or simply a smarter redistribution of the resources you already have.

Reclaiming Capacity Through Rightsizing#

Rightsizing often gets reduced to trimming resources off VMs. It’s really a continuous governance process. An oversized VM doesn’t just waste RAM or CPU: it reduces how effectively DRS can balance load, complicates placement during a failover, extends maintenance windows, creates NUMA constraints, narrows the number of hosts a workload can restart on, and distorts how much capacity actually remains.

Run it against enough historical data to be representative, accounting for peaks, seasonality, batch windows, month-end closes, backups, and expected growth. Downsizing a VM based on a single daily average is risky; leaving it permanently at twice the capacity it needs is just as wasteful. A mature process observes, classifies, proposes a change, gets sign-off from the application owner, executes, verifies, and is ready to roll back if needed, then updates the baseline.

The payoff isn’t just a better-utilized cluster. It’s a more predictable platform, and a predictable platform is easier to protect, patch, expand, and fund.

Broadcom reference: VCF Operations 9.1

Memory Tiering: More Capacity, Not “Free” Memory#

Memory Tiering is an ESX kernel feature that uses local NVMe devices as a secondary memory tier behind DRAM. The aim is to keep hotter pages in DRAM and place colder pages on the NVMe tier, increasing effective system capacity when memory is the real constraint and CPU still has headroom.

It should not be confused with conventional swapping or treated as a substitute for rightsizing. Performance, VM compatibility, NVMe device characteristics, and the relationship between active and consumed memory must be validated against the actual workload. Broadcom recommends evaluating it through resource-efficiency metrics and the operating considerations documented for the feature.

Broadcom references: Memory Tiering considerations and best practices · Using Memory Tiering for Resource Efficiency

Reusing Hardware, Assigning It the Right Role#

Reusing existing hardware doesn’t mean ignoring compatibility, supportability, or lifecycle. It means separating a server’s remaining technical value from the role it was assigned in the past. A host may no longer be the right fit for the most demanding workloads and still be perfectly adequate for the Management Domain, for infrastructure services, for test environments, or for workloads with a predictable performance profile.

One option is to assign part of the existing hardware to domains or clusters with more predictable profiles and introduce new nodes for the most demanding workloads, keeping different hardware generations on separate lifecycles. This is not an automatic rule: the Management Domain is itself critical and must meet availability, capacity, compatibility, and support requirements. Reuse is therefore sound only when the bill of materials, the Broadcom Compatibility Guide, drivers, firmware, and the operating model confirm that the role is sustainable for the intended lifecycle.

Broadcom references: VMware Cloud Foundation 9.1 · Broadcom Compatibility Guide

Not Forcing Uniformity Across Different Generations#

One of the most common mistakes in consolidation projects is treating different hardware as if it were identical. Even servers from the same vendor but different generations can require different firmware, drivers, vendor add-ons, and BIOS settings, each with its own remediation cycle and update window.

With image-based lifecycle management, the goal isn’t to build one universal image at any cost. It’s to define consistent images for homogeneous clusters: one for the Management Domain, one for the Workload Domain, and additional ones where a cluster has particular hardware requirements. vSphere Lifecycle Manager manages host software, drivers, and firmware through exactly these images, and a well-thought-out segmentation reduces remediation complexity and the risk of pulling in incompatible packages. Heterogeneity doesn’t need to be eliminated on principle. It needs to be contained and governed.

Broadcom reference: Lifecycle Management in VMware Cloud Foundation

Choosing Storage Based on the Service, Not the Trend#

Adopting VMware Cloud Foundation doesn’t mean every piece of data has to sit on the same type of storage. The choice should be driven by performance requirements, protection, resilience, the operating model, available skills, expected growth, cost, and compatibility with the VCF domain. A mature architecture often runs a domain’s primary storage alongside supplementary storage, backup repositories, and replication targets, each with its own service class.

vSAN ESA can be the natural choice when you want an integrated, scalable, policy-managed platform, but only if it’s backed by a validated hardware bill of materials and a coherent design for network, capacity, and resilience. Using generically compatible components without a full validation produces a platform that works today but is hard to patch and support tomorrow: the technical ability to install a solution is not the same as the operational ability to keep running it for years. When reusing existing servers, verifying controller, NVMe device, NIC, firmware, and driver compatibility has to come before any budget decision.

vSAN ESA: Deduplication and Compression Without Overstating the Benefit#

VCF 9.1 introduces Global Deduplication for vSAN ESA and improves compression. These are important tools for reducing physical footprint, but efficiency depends on data content, policies, release requirements, and workload behavior. Already compressed, encrypted, or weakly repetitive data can produce very different results from templates, virtual desktops, or large sets of similar operating systems.

Capacity planning should therefore start with raw capacity, subtract protection, operational slack, and rebuild space, and only then apply a conservative reduction ratio based on observed data. Deduplication and compression can postpone expansion and improve cost per usable terabyte; they should not be used to remove the safety margin.

Broadcom references: What’s New in vSAN 9.1 · Using Deduplication and Compression in vSAN

Designing the Cluster for Failure, Not for Normal Operation#

A cluster shouldn’t be sized to work only when every host is available. It needs to keep the service running through the loss of a host, a maintenance window, an update, a component failure, a storage rebuild, or a site outage where the design calls for one. That’s why you should never allocate the full theoretical capacity: a six-host cluster doesn’t simply give you six times the capacity of a single node. You have to subtract the margin for N+1 (or better), hypervisor and management overhead, the capacity maintenance requires, margin for growth and peaks, and any capacity that storage protection policies demand.

In clusters spread across two fault domains, you also need to account for how the whole system behaves if you lose a site. The witness, placed in a third location or in the cloud, doesn’t add capacity for workloads; it participates in determining quorum correctly. This reframes the core question of capacity planning: it’s no longer how many VMs you can fit on the available hosts, but how many VMs you can keep running through the failure scenario you’ve committed to supporting.

Broadcom reference: VMware vSAN Stretched Cluster

Consolidating Without Creating a New Monolith#

Reducing the number of clusters brings real benefits: less fragmentation, more effective DRS, better use of capacity, fewer wasted reserves, a simpler lifecycle. But consolidating everything into a single cluster risks creating a monolith that’s hard to manage. The design has to balance two opposing needs: avoiding clusters that are too small and inefficient, and avoiding clusters that are too large and lack a clear operational boundary.

Those boundaries can come from hardware generation, security requirements, application licensing, performance profile, criticality, maintenance windows, location, or availability requirements. A cluster is a technical unit, but it’s also an operational boundary: large enough to use resources well, coherent enough to be managed as a single object.

Capacity Planning as a FinOps Function#

Infrastructure capacity isn’t just a technical topic. Every idle core, every ungoverned terabyte, every oversized VM has an economic effect. VCF Operations includes capacity and cost management capabilities that let you correlate infrastructure usage with the associated costs, licensing included, and this makes it possible to move past the traditional model of buying enough capacity upfront for the next five years, replacing it with measured growth, operational thresholds, forecast scenarios, and an explicit comparison between expansion, consolidation, and optimization.

Applying FinOps to a private cloud doesn’t mean charging every VM an internal invoice. It means making the link between a technical choice and its economic consequence visible. An oversized VM isn’t just technically inefficient: it occupies capacity that can force the organization to bring forward an investment that wouldn’t otherwise be needed yet.

Broadcom reference: VCF Operations 9.1

VCF Operations as a Decision System#

A traditional monitoring system answers the question of what isn’t working. A mature operations platform also needs to answer what’s about to run out, where recoverable capacity exists, which cluster will grow fastest, which VMs are oversized, which hosts are limiting the lifecycle, which investment can be postponed, and how much the current inefficiency is costing.

In VCF 9, Fleet Management centralizes the management of components and VCF instances registered in a fleet. Initial deployment and validation belong instead to VCF Installer workflows and the platform’s lifecycle tooling: they are related functions, but they are not interchangeable. The value does not come from the dashboard itself. It comes from a cycle that collects data, sets baselines, flags anomalies, forecasts growth, opens corrective actions, measures the outcome, and updates the capacity plan. Without that cycle, even the best operations platform risks becoming a set of charts people only open during an emergency.

Broadcom references: Fleet Management · VMware Cloud Foundation 9.1

Buying Only After Defining the Target Architecture#

Buying hardware ahead of time can lock you into constraints that last for years. Before ordering new servers, it’s worth having defined the Management and Workload Domains, the number and size of clusters, which hardware generations can be reused, the vLCM configuration, the storage choice, the network model, resilience requirements and fault domains, N+1 or N+2 capacity, expected growth, the core-to-memory ratio, possible use of Memory Tiering, conservative assumptions for vSAN ESA data reduction, and the observability strategy. Only after settling these points does it make sense to build the bill of materials.

This avoids two recurring mistakes: buying very powerful hardware that ends up underused because the bottleneck is somewhere else, or buying unbalanced nodes, with plenty of cores and not enough memory, or with local storage that doesn’t fit the intended vSAN model. You don’t get past the hardware crunch by buying faster. You get past it by buying later, with better data, inside a design that’s already defined.

A Possible Evolution Model#

For an infrastructure with hardware that’s still valid but not uniform, a pragmatic path can unfold across five phases. A first assessment inventories servers, firmware, drivers, and components, checks compatibility, analyzes actual capacity, identifies the bottleneck, kicks off rightsizing, and cleans up snapshots and workloads nobody needs anymore. Next comes the platform design: separating the Management and Workload Domains, defining clusters, choosing primary and supplementary storage, the network model, resilience criteria, and the vLCM strategy.

From there you move to controlled reuse, assigning existing hardware to compatible roles, keeping clusters homogeneous, and keeping lifecycle images separate. Targeted investment comes only after that: validated nodes for the most demanding workloads, vSAN ESA where it fits the requirements, a network upgrade toward 25 GbE where traffic patterns call for it, always in response to a bottleneck you’ve actually measured. The last phase is ongoing operations: capacity management, rightsizing, chargeback or showback, growth monitoring, coordinated lifecycle, and periodic review of thresholds.

The Decisions That Actually Save Hardware#

Real savings rarely come from a single feature. They come from a combination of consistent choices: separating physical capacity from usable capacity, sizing for failure, identifying the real bottleneck, reclaiming resources through rightsizing, consolidating clusters that are too small, keeping different hardware generations and lifecycles separate, reusing hosts in the roles that fit them, aligning storage and network with the service, tying capacity to cost, and buying only what the architecture demonstrates you actually need.

Sometimes the assessment will confirm you need new servers. Other times it will show that compute still has room and the investment should go into storage, or it will let you postpone the purchase entirely by reclaiming hidden capacity through rightsizing, consolidation, Memory Tiering, and data reduction where measurements prove their effectiveness. All three outcomes are legitimate. The goal isn’t to avoid new hardware at all costs. It’s to avoid buying it to compensate for a problem you haven’t actually understood yet.

Conclusions#

VMware Cloud Foundation 9.1 can be the starting point for a more integrated, governable, and predictable private cloud, but the biggest benefit doesn’t come from installing the platform itself. It comes from changing how infrastructure decisions get made: capacity stops being a static quantity you buy in advance and becomes something you measure, protect, reclaim, distribute, and govern over time.

Existing hardware shouldn’t be kept indiscriminately or replaced automatically: it should be evaluated and assigned to the role where it can keep producing value. New hardware, when you genuinely need it, should eliminate a measured limit, not a guess. In a mature private cloud, optimization isn’t a temporary measure while you wait for the next purchase. It’s part of the architecture, just like storage or the network.
